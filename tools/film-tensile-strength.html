What this tool solves

Peak force alone is not a fair comparison when film strips have different original cross sections. This calculator computes peak engineering stress and elongation at break separately, retaining each specimen so you can see whether one reading is driving the summary.

How to choose the inputs

Use the original strip width and measured film thickness, not the roll width or nominal gauge code. Enter the original gauge length that corresponds to the measured extension. Use extension, not the final stretched length, and record machine-direction and transverse-direction specimens in separate runs.

How the calculation works

Original cross section in mm² is width in mm multiplied by thickness in µm divided by 1,000. Divide the peak force in newtons by that area for MPa. Divide extension at break by original gauge length and multiply by 100 for percent elongation at break.

Worked example

A 15 mm × 50 µm strip has a 0.75 mm² original cross section. A 30 N peak gives 40 MPa. A 20 mm extension on a 50 mm initial gauge length gives 40% elongation at break. The three example rows produce means of 40 MPa and 40%.

How to interpret the output

Peak stress describes the largest measured load normalized to the original section. Elongation describes how far the strip stretched at break. The two events need not coincide. Review thickness, direction and the displacement measurement basis before comparing runs.

Common mistakes

  • Do not divide film tension by strip width alone; that gives force per width, not stress.
  • Subtract the original gauge length if your record contains final length rather than extension.
  • Do not infer Young’s modulus or yield stress from peak force and extension at break.

Assumptions and limitations

This worksheet does not identify a valid break from a trace, compensate for grip slip or establish ASTM D882 compliance. Do not use this result as a load rating or a substitute for the lab report.
